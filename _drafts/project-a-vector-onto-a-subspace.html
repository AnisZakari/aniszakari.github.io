Let's be clear about the notation I'll use. It is important to understand that beta is a vector, not a scalar. Beta is the vector of the coordinates of Vx

It is very tempting to try getting thoses betas by projecting V on each one of the vetors that spans X. Yeah ! let's just project v on x1 and on x2 and I'm done, I have the coordinates of vx :)

It sure would work if x1 and x2 form an orthogonal basis but unfortunatly in the problems you'll encounter in ML, it's almost never the case.
Instead we want to rely on a technique that works everytime, no matter how X is formed.

Let's focus on the things that will always hold true.

As we are projection v perpendicularly to X, the vector (v - vx) will always be orthogonal to v or to the subspace X (because v lays on X).

Notice that we chose to use the fact that X is perpendicular to (v - vx) rather than the fact that vx is perpendicular to (v - vx) to avoid having twice vx in the equation. Indeed vx contains the unknown beta so it would be clumsy to use an unknown when you can use X which is known.
